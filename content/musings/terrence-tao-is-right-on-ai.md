title: 'Terrence Tao Is Right about AI'

For those of us whose research interests lie at the intersection of Mathematics
and Computer Science, most of us have heard of the OpenAI Millennial Prize
controversy which erupted violently last week.

For those of us who were living under a rock, on September 7, OpenAI published a
paper claiming that they had solved the Navier-Stokes equation on fluid
dynamics, one of the Millennium Prize equations as named by the Clay Mathematics
Institute. This announcement has courted massive controversy, as OpenAI has been
accused by NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster of plagiarizing his and Levent
Alpöge's research work on the equation to make this alleged breakthrough.

However, while the big conversation is surrounding how OpenAI can use data (by
Tristan's own admission he used ChatGPT to help him write his paper), the bigger
question is LLMs now have the potential to solve complicated mathematical
problems, such as Millenium Prize Equations, which begs the question: How do
LLMs assist in scientific discovery?

As always, Terrence Tao has some opinions on all of this.

Terry recently appeared as a guest on Big Think to describe what he refers to as
"The Paradox at the heart of AI and science". In his guest appearance, he argues
that if we automate certain parts of the discovery and struggle of mathematics,
that we run the very high risk of missing some other important aspect of the
problem. His example is how a lot of influential discoveries in science and math
were discovered more so "on accident" when people were trying to solve other
problems. This is an important fact to note: that the process of discovery is
not linear. To be fair, it doesn't even follow any sort of "function" because
it's a constantly evolving and ongoing process where backtracking isn't an
outlier, it's to be expected.

This is where the paradox of AI and science exists, because AI enables an
acceleration of "brute forcing" the solutions, which means that we may miss out
on scientific discoveries because we're chasing the wrong thing. It also makes
it possible that we trade speed for "brain debt" where we can't explain the
phenomena at all, we just kind of "assume it's true because the computer said
so". This goes against the entire concept of science to a certain extent, due to

Computer Science and Software Engineering has a phenomena we refer to as
"technical debt", where because we offload parts of our code to other people who
have written basic code to accomplish them, we exchange knowledge and
understanding of our codebases for speed of deployment. AI introduces the risk
of what we're starting to call "cognitive debt" where we literally don't
understand how it works, all we do know is that it works.

And I don't think that's a world that scientists, or us for that matter, want to
be in.
